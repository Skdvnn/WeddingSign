Painting it on wood

The part that actually determines whether this looks great or homemade. Written for a hand-painted panel, not a vinyl decal.

Pick your typeface for the brush, not the screen

  • Monoline / uniform-stroke faces are forgiving. Anton, Bebas Neue, Archivo Black, Oswald and Space Mono hold a near-constant stroke width, so a wobble of a millimetre disappears. These are directions 01–04.
  • High-contrast serifs are unforgiving. Playfair and Bodoni swing from a fat stem to a hairline. Hairlines on wood grain skip, break up, and read as sloppy rather than delicate. If you love direction 08, paint the thick strokes with a flat brush and pull the hairlines with a signwriter's liner brush or a Posca PC-1MR, not the same brush.
  • Cap-height sanity check: for a sign read at ~20 feet, letters want to be at least 2 in tall; at 40 feet, 4 in. Your main names should be the only thing that passes that test — everything else is intentionally small.

Mustard ground vs. mustard lettering

 Mustard ground, dark typeRaw wood, mustard type
Coverage2–3 rolled coats to kill the grain, then letters. More paint, more drying time.One clear coat, then letters. Much faster.
ContrastExcellent. Near-black on #C4A939 is a very high ratio — reads across a lawn.Weak. Mustard on tan wood is low contrast and will muddy in shade or at dusk.
MistakesEasy — touch out with the mustard, it's opaque.Hard — you can't touch out over bare wood without a visible patch.
Best forAny of the eight. This is the safer bet.Only 01–03, where the letters are fat enough to survive low contrast.
  • The compromise, if you want the wood to show: paint a mustard panel — a large rectangle or arch inset a few inches from the edges — and leave a raw wood border around it. You get the grain and the contrast. Direction 05 is built for exactly this.

Getting the letters onto the board

  • Projector (best results): export your chosen layout as a PDF at the real panel size, throw it on the board from a cheap projector in a dark room, trace with a hard pencil. Straight, square, no distortion.
  • Print-and-transfer: tile the layout across letter-size sheets, tape into position, and either rub graphite on the back of the print or run a chalk-covered transfer sheet under it.
  • Do not freehand the baseline. Snap a chalk line or run low-tack tape for every baseline before you draw a single letter. Crooked baselines are the single most common tell.

Paint & finish

  • Sand to 220, then prime — bare wood drinks paint unevenly and mustard is a weak-pigment colour that will go blotchy without it.
  • Acrylic craft or sign enamel over latex. Two thin coats on the lettering beats one thick one; thick coats leave a ridge that catches light and shows every wobble.
  • Seal with a matte or satin topcoat. Gloss on a big flat panel throws glare and every photo of it will have a hotspot in the middle.
  • If it's outdoors: exterior-grade sealer, and back-brace the panel. A 24×36 board left in the sun will cup.
  • Do a test board. Same wood, same primer, one word in the real typeface at the real size. Twenty minutes now saves the whole panel later.

Common sizes

  • 18 × 24 in — small, works on an easel beside a door.
  • 24 × 36 in — the standard welcome-sign size and what these layouts are proportioned for (5:7 is close).
  • 30 × 40 in or larger — statement piece; needs a heavier easel and a second pair of hands.
