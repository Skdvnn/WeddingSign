Ground & lettering

Decided: solid mustard #BFA52B with black lettering. The panel is already painted — the notes below are kept for reference only.

What the reception photo settles

  • Black bentwood chairs, blush linen, mustard napkins. Black lettering is now the obvious call — it's already the strongest colour in the room, and the sign matching the chairs ties the whole thing together.
  • Your napkin is almost exactly Paint A (#BFA52B). Paint B (#D0C82F) is brighter and greener — closer to the chartreuse in that first poster you sent than to anything at the reception. Worth deciding whether you want the sign to match the napkins or deliberately sit a step brighter than them.
  • Blush linen is now a ground option. It's warmer and pinker than the bone in your stationery — using it makes the sign a reception object rather than a stationery object. Both are defensible; bone ties to the invite, blush ties to the tables.
  • Mustard lettering on blush is about 3.6:1 — too low. If you want mustard type, put it on birch and keep it fat.

  • Short version: black on birch or bone if you want it to feel like the suite; white on denim or ink if you want the sign to be the moment. Skip the stain.
  • White lettering is harder to paint than black. White is a weak-covering pigment — it takes three thin coats over a dark ground to stop looking grey and streaky, and every ragged edge shows more than it does in black. Budget extra time, or use a white paint pen for details and a brush only for the big serif.

The compromise worth considering

  • Paint a bone or mustard field inset a few inches from the edges, leaving a raw birch border. You get the plywood — which is genuinely the nicest material here — plus a forgiving surface to letter on. Directions 02, 05, 07 and 08 all take an inset field without changing the layout.
  • Leave the ply edge exposed. Birch ply's laminated edge is the best thing about it. Don't band it, don't paint it, just sand and seal it.

Painting on smooth ply, specifically

  • Birch face veneer is thin. Sand 180 then 220, no coarser — go heavier and you'll cut through to the glue line, which shows as a grey patch you can't fix.
  • Bare birch drinks unevenly and will blotch. One coat of dewaxed shellac or clear sanding sealer before any lettering, sanded back lightly with 320.
  • Because the surface is smooth, a paint pen is viable here in a way it isn't on rough wood. Posca PC-1M / PC-3M for the mono details, brush for the big serif. Test on an offcut — some pens bleed into sealed birch.
  • Two thin coats on lettering, not one thick. Thick edges catch raking light and every photo picks it up.
  • Matte or satin topcoat only. Gloss on a large flat panel means a hotspot in the middle of every photo.

Why the stencil wins

  • A projector only gives you an accurate outline. Knowing where the letters go was never the difficulty — cutting a clean edge on a 3 mm hairline with a brush is. A stencil removes that problem entirely, which matters most for Instrument Serif.
  • Send a vector PDF or SVG, not a screenshot. Print this page to PDF at panel size and name the font — Instrument Serif is free on Google Fonts, so any shop can set it.
  • Ask for the counters on transfer tape. The holes in A, o, e and S are loose islands in a stencil. A good supplier positions them for you; if they don't, you're placing them by eye and it will show.
  • Seal the stencil edge with the ground colour first. Lay the stencil, brush a thin coat of the background colour over all the edges, let it dry, then apply the lettering colour. Any bleed is background-on-background and invisible. This single step is the difference between crisp and fuzzy.
  • Dab, don't stroke. Foam pouncer or stencil brush held vertical, tapped. A loaded brush dragged sideways forces paint under the edge. Two thin coats.
  • Peel while slightly soft, shallow angle, back on itself. Cured paint tears along the line.
  • Test the adhesive on an offcut. On raw or washed ply vinyl can lift wood fibres when pulled. On primed or sealed ground it's fine. Going raw-birch, ask for low-tack stencil vinyl and burnish gently.

Either way

  • Chalk-line or tape every baseline before anything goes down. Crooked baselines are the one mistake that reads as amateur from across a garden.
  • Cap-height check: at ~20 ft letters want 2 in minimum, at ~40 ft 4 in. Only the names should pass that — the mono details are meant to be read up close.
  • Ruth Bancroft is outdoors and Walnut Creek in September gets hot. Back-brace the panel or 3/4 in ply will still cup in an afternoon of direct sun.

  • The mono reads as Roboto Mono with heavy letterspacing (0.13em–0.2em). Whatever detail face you land on, keep the tracking wide — it's a big part of why the suite feels considered rather than default.
  • The one to notice: EB Garamond is the sleeper pick. Old-style proportions, near-even stroke weight, so it reads as traditional and formal while being one of the most forgiving faces here to paint. Cormorant looks similar on screen and is the opposite — far too fine.
  • The heavy grotesque on your save-the-date is Helvetica-family; Inter Bold at tight tracking is the free stand-in, used in direction 03. That one stays fixed regardless of the dropdowns, since it's deliberately quoting the STD.
  • Stroke contrast is the whole game for hand-painting. Bodoni and Cormorant swing from a fat stem to a hairline — on a brush, the hairlines break up and read as sloppy rather than delicate. Anton, Archivo and EB Garamond hold a near-constant width, so a millimetre of wobble vanishes.

Building the A-frame

Two 3/4 in birch project panels, 24×36 in (2×3 ft), hinged at the top into a sandwich board. Written for the tools you already have: rollers, brushes, hinges, circular saw.

Read this one first — it changes the layout

  • A 36 in A-frame leans back about 15°, so your top edge lands near 35 in off the ground. Adult eye level is roughly 60 in. Guests will be looking down at this sign, not at it.
  • That inverts the usual hierarchy. Anything in the bottom third sits at knee height and effectively won't be read — so the fine mono details in layouts 01, 05 and 08 need to move up, not stay at the base.
  • Two fixes, pick one. Either compress the whole composition into the top two-thirds and leave the bottom third empty, or set the frame on a low riser / crate to lift it 12–18 in. The riser is less work and looks deliberate.
  • Layout 07 (Ledger) is the exception — it's meant to be read up close at walking pace, so knee-height detail is fine. It's the best fit for an A-frame of the eight.

You get two faces — use them

  • A sandwich board has two outward faces. Front: the welcome (01, 02, 03 or 05). Back: the order of the day (07), or directions — ceremony this way / bar inside. Same type system, no extra sign to build.
  • Paint both faces even if the back stays blank. An unfinished face will absorb moisture at a different rate than the finished one and the panel will cup toward the sealed side. This is the single most common failure on a home-built board.

Cut & prep

  • Check the panels are actually square before anything else — measure both diagonals, they should match within 1/16 in. Pre-cut project panels often aren't. Trim with the circular saw against a clamped straightedge if needed.
  • Circular saw on birch ply tears out on the up side of the blade. Cut with the good face down, use a fine-tooth (40T+) plywood blade, and score the cut line with a utility knife first. A strip of painter's tape along the line also helps.
  • Sand faces 150 → 180 → 220. Don't go coarser than 150 — birch face veneer is thin and you'll cut through to the glue line, which shows as a grey patch that no amount of paint hides.
  • Ease every edge and corner with 220 wrapped on a block. Sharp ply edges hold no paint and will chip the first time it's loaded into a car.
  • Ply edges drink paint like a straw. Hit all four edges of both panels with thinned primer or wood filler first, sand flat, then prime again. Skipping this is why home-built signs have fuzzy edges.

Paint

  • Use a high-density foam roller, not a nap roller. Nap leaves orange-peel texture on smooth ply; 4 in foam gives you a near-sprayed finish. Save the brushes for lettering and edges.
  • Order: prime both faces and all edges → sand 320 → two thin ground coats, sanding lightly between → transfer the layout → letter → topcoat.
  • Roll in one direction, then finish with a single light pass in the other ("laying off") before it tacks. Don't rework a roller mark once it's starting to set — you'll tear it.
  • If you're leaving raw birch: dewaxed shellac or clear sanding sealer instead of primer, sanded back with 320. Bare birch blotches badly without it.
  • Matte or satin topcoat only. Gloss on two large flat panels means a hotspot in the middle of every photo, and this thing will be photographed a lot.

Washing the wood

  • What it is: paint thinned far enough that it stains rather than covers, so the birch figure reads straight through the colour. It's the single best-looking option on ply — you get colour and the material, instead of choosing.
  • The mix: roughly 1 part paint : 3–4 parts water for acrylic or latex. Start thinner than you think and build. Each coat roughly doubles the depth, and you cannot take it back off.
  • Seal the wood FIRST or the wash will blotch. This is the opposite of normal painting instinct and it's the step people skip. Bare birch has hard and soft grain that drink at wildly different rates. One coat of dilute shellac (a "washcoat"), sanded back with 320, evens the absorption. Skip it and you get a leopard-print panel.
  • Apply with a rag or a wide brush, not a roller. Long strokes running the full length of the panel in one pass, following the grain direction. Wipe back with a clean cloth while wet to control depth.
  • Never stop mid-panel. A wash shows every lap mark where wet meets dry. Work fast, keep a wet edge, do the whole face in one go.
  • Both faces, same number of coats. Water-based wash raises grain and swells the veneer — unbalanced and the panel cups. Sand very lightly with 320 after the first coat once the raised grain dries.
  • Lettering over a wash is harder, because the surface is now more absorbent than a sealed painted ground. Seal over the dried wash with matte clear before you letter, or the paint will feather into the grain.
  • Test the strength on an offcut. The wash slider in the toggles is a preview, not a recipe — dilution to on-panel depth is not linear, and wet always looks darker than dry.

Multi-tone panels (17–20)

  • Tape line quality is the whole job. Use good tape, burnish the edge down hard with a fingernail or a card, then seal the tape edge with a thin coat of the base colour first. That coat bleeds under the tape instead of your accent, so the accent line comes out surgical.
  • Pull tape while the paint is still slightly soft, at a shallow angle back on itself. Fully cured paint tears along the line.
  • Blocks and bands are easier than they look and read far stronger from distance than coloured type does — a solid field is visible at eighty feet, letterforms aren't.
  • For the arch (20), a pencil on a string pinned at the centre gives you a clean semicircle. Tape the two verticals, freehand nothing.
  • The block wants to be the curry. Your reception palette is black, blush and mustard — a colour block in anything outside that reads as an import. The bands in 17, 18 and 20 default to whichever mustard isn't the current ground.
  • Three colours is the ceiling, and you're better at two. Birch or blush ground, curry block, black type. That's the whole thing.

Lettering

  • Project it. Print this page to PDF, project onto the panel in a dark room, trace with a hard (2H) pencil. Ten minutes, perfectly square, no distortion. Every other transfer method is worse.
  • Chalk-line or tape every baseline before you draw a letter. Crooked baselines are the one mistake that reads as amateur from thirty feet.
  • Two thin coats on the letters, not one thick. Thick edges leave a ridge that catches raking light and shows every wobble in photos.
  • Because sealed birch is smooth, paint pens actually work here — Posca PC-1M/PC-3M for the mono details, brush for the big serif. Test on an offcut; some pens feather on sealed ply.
  • Do a test board. An offcut, same prep, one word at full size in Instrument Serif. Twenty minutes now tells you whether those hairlines are going to fight you before you've committed the real panel.

Hinging

  • Use a continuous (piano) hinge, not butt hinges — 24 in to match the panel width. It spreads the load across the whole top edge and stops the frame racking side to side, which a pair of butt hinges won't.
  • Mount it surface-mounted across the two outer faces at the top edge, not into the 3/4 in edge itself. Screws into plywood edge-grain strip out. Surface-mounting also means the panels close face-to-face, protecting your artwork in transit.
  • Screws no longer than 5/8 in into 3/4 in stock. Pilot-drill every hole — birch ply splits, and there is no fixing a split at the hinge line.
  • Hinge after the topcoat has cured, not before. Mask the hinge footprint if you want paint-free metal-to-wood contact.
  • Fit a spreader. Light chain or a nylon strap between eye screws on the inner faces, about 8 in up from the bottom. Set it so the feet sit 20–24 in apart — roughly a 35° spread. Without it the frame will slowly splay on grass and eventually do the splits.

Outdoors at Ruth Bancroft

  • Weight: two 3/4 in birch panels at 24 × 36 come to roughly 31 lb total. Heavy to carry, but that mass is what keeps it upright — don't be tempted into thinner stock.
  • It's still a 12 sq ft sail. Walnut Creek gets an afternoon breeze in September; drop a sandbag or two bricks inside the A where nobody sees them.
  • Seal the bottom edges twice. They'll sit on irrigated ground and wick moisture straight up into the plies. This is how the bottom inch of a sign goes black.
  • Exterior-grade topcoat, and don't set it out days early. September sun on a dark painted panel gets hot enough to move the wood.
  • Glue a strip of scrap or rubber feet along each bottom edge to widen the footprint on soft ground and keep the ply off wet soil.
